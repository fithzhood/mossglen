## On the three gates

**Gate 1 — `shareOfWindow` no higher than 0.20 in any milestone window. NOT MET (max 0.38). I void the gate, and the defence is better than the developer stated it.**

The developer's account — windows shrank from ~730 to ~279 with the groves reverted, so the same behaviour divides by a smaller denominator — is true but is not what actually happened. Ten of the 45 windows exceed 0.20. Their worst repetitions are **2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 7, 7 and 7 actions**, in windows of 6 to 32 progress actions. The 0.38 maximum is literally three mushroom donations inside an 8-action window (seed 55555, `stage:4`). Meanwhile the single most repetitive window in the entire file — 55 berry-picks in seed 888001's `built:steppingstones` — scores exactly 0.20 and **passes**. The gate is not merely miscalibrated; on this data it is inversely correlated with the defect it exists to catch. Its author designed it to be "immune to window length" and it is precisely the opposite: it is hypersensitive to short ones.

**Can a gate calibrated on a reverted version bind this one?** No — and I will give the principle rather than the convenience, because the convenience runs the other way for the developer here. A gate is a proxy for a defect. This one was calibrated as "hold-the-line at v004's 0.19" against window lengths that existed only in a build the loop then deleted. Applying it to v005 does not test whether repetition worsened; it tests whether the windows got shorter, which they did, because the arbiter who wrote the gate rejected the version that lengthened them. Enforcing it would mean the loop rejects a version for a side effect of its own previous rejection — the same rule-eating-itself failure my predecessor correctly named when it voided v003's `worstAbsoluteRepetition < 55` pre-commitment. A gate whose baseline no longer exists cannot fire as an automatic rejection. It can and does inform the score, and it has: B is not a 5.

What I do **not** accept is the framing that "absolute repetition fell". It did not fall. It was restored. See gate 2.

**Gate 2 — `mostRepeatedActionOfAnyKind` must not exceed 290. MET, at 65, and worth nothing.** It is 65 in seed 888001's `built:steppingstones` window, and the action is `talk:marla`. That is v003's number, in v003's seed, in v003's milestone, on v003's action, to the unit. `worstAbsoluteRepetition` is likewise 55 in the same seed / milestone / action with the same 279-action window and the same 0.2 share. Against the only baseline that now exists, both figures moved **zero**. The gate is met because the code that produced 290 was deleted, not because anyone worked on repetition. Credited as met; credited with nothing.

**Gate 3 — add `mostRepeatedActionPerInGameDay` so the figure stops scaling with arc length. NOT MET. The field does not exist.** I searched the whole file: the only string matching `PerInGameDay` is `config.realMinutesPerInGameDay`. This is the cheapest of the three conditions — a reporting field, not a gameplay change — and it is the one that would have made gates 1 and 2 unnecessary, because it measures directly the thing both were failing proxies for. It was skipped in a version whose entire declared scope was two axes it does not touch, which is exactly the version that had room for it. This is the third time in the series a pure-measurement condition has been ignored inside a version that could afford it (v003 did the same on the grind rule). Not an automatic rejection, because it was not stated as one. **Binding for v006: report `mostRepeatedActionPerInGameDay` with a target under 8, and restore the plain-reading grind list alongside the conjunct one. A v006 that reports neither is a rejection on the grind veto, because at that point the file will contain no test of repetition that is capable of failing.**

## Verdict

**ACCEPTED.** 20/25. The ratchet clears at exactly the floor and no veto fires.

Say plainly what this acceptance is. It is not an endorsement. Every one of the five axes reads the number v003 read, on a game that is structurally v003 plus a dialogue pool, a mat, a highlight ellipse and 22 fewer clipped pixels. The version did what the rejection demanded — it repaired C and D — and it did so on the two most decidable, most checkable, cheapest-to-verify axes in the rubric, which is exactly what a loop under a total-based ratchet will learn to do if nobody says otherwise. I am saying otherwise: a second consecutive version that clears by fixing pixels and text counts while `utilisationPct.moss` reads 79.4 for a fourth time does not clear.

What earns the acceptance beyond the arithmetic is one thing the developer was not asked for and did anyway: the dialogue pool grew 31% (147 → 192) and per-playthrough reach went **up** in every single seed while it grew. That metric had fallen twice in a row and was the subject of a standing complaint; it is the first unambiguous, unrequested improvement in the series that cost real work. It is why B holds at 4 rather than slipping.

What the acceptance does not cover, in order: the back half (fourth version, unstarted or reverted); a reporting field that was skipped because it was free; a grind rule narrowed back to a test that cannot fail; and a version that fixed the *count* in the dialogue sample without fixing the *silence* the count was standing in for.

## The single most important weakness

**The arrow from decorating to villagers — pillar 2 feeding pillar 3, the half of the loop that makes a cozy game feel inhabited rather than administered — exists in the code and does not reach a player, and three independent artifacts show it.** *It is absent from the writing:* the fourteen-line sample spans days 1 to 38 and therefore crosses `decorated` (day 1–3 in every seed), `home:full` (day 1–4), `built:bench`, `built:flowerbed`, `built:pondlantern` and `built:green` — and contains **zero** lines naming an item, a piece of furniture, a project or the house; eleven of fourteen are pure time-of-day or self-description ambience. *The data says exactly which lines are failing:* the reactive families are the ones concentrated in `dialogueNeverSeen` — `marla.room.water` missed by 4 of 5 seeds, `pim.room.water` and `bodkin.room.water` by 3 of 5, `marla.home.feather` and `marla.home.reed` by 2, `bodkin.home.glassbead` by 1 — so the lines are *written*, they simply do not fire, and reach rising to 96.14% overall did not touch them because the misses are structural, not statistical. *And the mechanism is already diagnosed:* v003 identified it precisely — stages 2, 3 and 4 all complete on days 1–3, so `marla.stage.2` is superseded before the player next talks to anyone, and it has now been missed by 4 of 5 seeds for **five consecutive versions**. The same defect explains D's distribution problem, which is not a coincidence: Marla owns the three least-reachable lines in the file *and* gets 1 slot of 14 in the sample, because she is the villager whose lines are mostly reactive, and a system that drops reactions drops her. **The fix is one mechanism and it closes A, B and D at once: queue reactions instead of firing them.** When the player places an object, completes a project, or advances a stage, push a line onto a per-villager queue that survives supersession and is delivered ahead of ambient lines on the next conversation with that villager; ambient lines become the fallback rather than the default, which also breaks up the six-Pim-lines-in-a-row pattern. Success is measurable in fields already in the file, with no new instrumentation: minimum per-seed `dialoguePct` ≥ 97 (currently 93.2), no defined line missed by more than one seed in five (currently three lines missed by four), Marla at ≥ 3 of 14 sample slots (currently 1), and **≥ 3 of the 14 sample lines naming something the player gathered, placed or built (currently 0)**.
